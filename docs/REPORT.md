## Abstract

Voice cloning has made the fake-relative and fake-official phone scam cheap to
run at scale, and the two research communities that could stop it are looking
at different halves of the problem. Audio anti-spoofing decides whether a voice
was generated by a machine, which is not the same question as whether a call is
a fraud, because plenty of legitimate calls are synthetic. Text scam
classification decides whether words are coercive, which fails on a human
scammer improvising and on a cloned voice reading something innocuous. This
work builds a system that runs both branches over Hindi-English code-mixed
telephone audio and combines them, and it argues that the interesting part is
not the combination itself but three signals that only exist once both
modalities are present: the mismatch between how threatening the words are and
how animated the voice is, the trajectory of coercive pressure across turns,
and which of the two languages carries the fraud vocabulary. The system reports
a calibrated risk score with per-feature attributions and highlighted
transcript spans, and it re-scores after every turn so that detection latency
is measurable rather than assumed.

## 1. Problem statement

Given an audio recording of a telephone call, decide whether the call is
fraudulent, and justify the decision.

The system must work at telephone bandwidth (8 kHz, band-limited, codec
degraded), on code-mixed Hindi-English speech, and must produce evidence a
human reviewer can check. Two labels matter and they are independent: whether
the voice is synthetic, and whether the conversation is a scam.

## 2. Motivation

Cyber-fraud losses reported to India's I4C crossed eleven thousand crore rupees
in FY2024, and the advisory traffic from RBI, TRAI and I4C through 2023 to 2025
is dominated by three script families: fake KYC and account-freeze calls, OTP
extraction, and the "digital arrest" scam in which a caller impersonating
police or CBI keeps a victim on a video call for hours. Voice cloning from a
few seconds of reference audio makes the fake-relative variant trivially
scalable.

The defences deployed against this are one-eyed in a specific and fixable way.
Section 3 sets out the gap; section 4 is the response.

## 3. Related work and the gap

| Work | Contribution | What it does not do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ASVspoof 2019 LA (Todisco et al., INTERSPEECH 2019) | The benchmark for logical-access spoofing, with LFCC-GMM and CQCC-GMM baselines | Clean studio audio, English, no notion of intent |
| ASVspoof 2021 (Yamagishi et al.) | Adds codec and compression conditions, directly relevant to telephony | Still audio only |
| RawNet2 (Tak et al., ICASSP 2021) | End-to-end raw-waveform anti-spoofing with a SincConv front end | No explanation, no text |
| AASIST (Jung et al., ICASSP 2022) | Spectro-temporal graph attention, strong results | Same |
| In-the-Wild (Mueller et al., INTERSPEECH 2022) | Shows lab-trained detectors degrade badly on real-world audio | English, no conversational dimension |
| Vishing and phishing text classification | Urgency and intent classification from text works well | Monolingual, not fused with audio authenticity |
| i-vector and x-vector speaker verification (Dehak 2011, Snyder 2018) | Speaker embeddings for identity checks | Not applied to scam screening |

The gap is threefold. No public system fuses audio authenticity with
conversational intent. Nothing targets Hinglish telephone speech. And nothing
produces the reviewable evidence output that a deployed fraud system would
need in order to be trusted or appealed.

## 4. Method

### 4.1 Architecture

Audio at 8 kHz enters a channel model (G.711 companding implemented exactly in
numpy, GSM and AMR-NB through ffmpeg where available, optional additive noise
at a set SNR), then splits.

The **voice branch** extracts five cepstral front ends implemented from
scratch: MFCC, GFCC over a gammatone filterbank, LFCC over a linear
filterbank, a constant-Q approximation for CQCC, and LPCC through
autocorrelation and Levinson-Durbin. Frames are pooled by mean, standard
deviation, skewness and kurtosis, including over first differences. Skew and
kurtosis are included because synthetic speech has a less heavy-tailed
coefficient distribution, which the first two moments do not capture.
Classifiers are a two-GMM log-likelihood ratio (the ASVspoof LFCC-GMM
baseline), gradient boosting on the pooled vectors, and a small SincConv
RawNet-style network. A TDNN speaker embedder supplies within-call embedding
consistency.

The **text branch** takes a transcript, from Whisper where available and from
the corpus gold otherwise. Per-token language identification separates
romanised Hindi from English. A linear-chain CRF implemented from scratch
(forward-backward in log space, analytic gradient, L-BFGS, Viterbi decoding)
tags seven fraud entity types. A BiLSTM-CRF tagger provides the neural
comparison. Intent is scored by a TF-IDF model over word and character n-grams
and by an interpretable regex and lexicon baseline.

The **cross-modal layer** is where the contribution sits, and it is described
below.

Twenty-five features feed a calibrated logistic regression. Because the model
is linear in standardised features, its Shapley values have a closed form, so
the evidence panel reports exact attributions rather than a sampled estimate.

### 4.2 Prosody-Intent Mismatch

For each caller turn, lexical arousal is computed from weighted fraud lexicons
(urgency, threat, authority, payment, personal-information request, isolation,
with benign markers subtracting) and blended with the intent model's score.
Acoustic arousal is computed from pitch coefficient of variation, pitch range
relative to mean, short-time energy variation and speaking-rate variation,
each mapped through fixed absolute anchors rather than per-call normalisation.
The anchors are absolute on purpose: normalising within a call would make a
uniformly flat synthetic call look normal, which is the exact case the feature
exists to catch.

The score combines a pressure-weighted gap with the correlation between the
two traces:

    gap         = sum_i lex_i * max(0, lex_i - aco_i) / sum_i lex_i
    incoherence = (1 - pearson(lex, aco)) / 2
    PIM         = 0.65 * gap + 0.35 * incoherence * max_i(lex_i)

The correlation term is scaled by peak lexical pressure so that a low
correlation on a call with no coercive language contributes nothing, which
would otherwise be noise rather than evidence.

On the module's own controlled test, identical wording delivered flat scores
0.474 and delivered with human prosody scores 0.048.

### 4.3 Coercion trajectory

Each turn is assigned a dialogue act from an eighteen-act set, and each act
carries a hand-set pressure rank taken from the script structure described in
public advisories. The features are the fitted slope of rank against turn
index over caller turns, the peak rank reached, the log-likelihood ratio of
the act sequence under first-order Markov models fitted separately on scam and
benign calls, and the fraction of callee turns that resist or question. The
ranks are hand-set rather than learned because the slope has to remain
readable in the evidence panel; a learned ranking would score better and
explain worse.

### 4.4 Code-switching profile

Per-token language tags yield the Gamback and Das code-mixing index, the
switch-point count, the entropy of the language-transition distribution, and
the share of fraud-entity tokens carried by English. The last is the novel
one. Indian fraud scripts keep threats and emotional pressure in Hindi while
the financial and legal vocabulary stays English, so the alignment between
entity type and carrier language is itself discriminative.

### 4.5 Streaming and time to detection

The whole stack re-runs on the prefix heard so far after every turn. The first
crossing of the alert threshold gives the time to detection in seconds and in
turns. Per-turn prosody and the voice branch are cached across prefixes, which
turns a quadratic cost into a linear one.

### 4.6 CallForge, the corpus generator

A template grammar over ten scam and eight benign scenarios produces dialogues
whose BIO entity tags, dialogue acts and per-token language tags are emitted by
the slots that produced the tokens, so the gold annotation is exact by
construction and cannot drift. Speakers are partitioned across splits before
calls are assigned to speakers, which is what keeps the splits honest.

Three call styles exist deliberately. Benign scenarios include hard negatives
that are lexically close to scams: a genuine bank reminder mentioning KYC, a
real delivery call asking the customer to read out an OTP, an electricity
notice with a due date. Scam scenarios include a mild style that asks one
innocuous-sounding verification question, which the text branch is expected to
miss and the voice branch is expected to catch.

Audio comes from a source-filter synthesiser: a glottal pulse train through
formant resonators. The human setting adds cycle-to-cycle pitch jitter,
amplitude shimmer, breath noise and slow pitch drift; the synthetic setting
suppresses them and over-smooths the spectral envelope. Measured on the
generated audio, synthetic renderings show roughly seven times lower jitter and
about half the spectral-flatness variance of the human renderings, which are
the artefacts the anti-spoofing features are designed to find. This is a
simulator for offline work and is documented as one: it is not a clone of any
person, and the real-data results come from the Colab notebooks.

## 5. Experimental setup

- 480 calls, balanced across the four cells of {human, cloned} x {benign, scam}
  and across scenarios, with speaker-disjoint train, dev and test splits.
- All audio at 8 kHz. Channel conditions: clean, G.711 mu-law, G.711 A-law,
  GSM, AMR-NB. Where ffmpeg is absent the GSM and AMR paths fall back to a
  documented numpy approximation and are labelled as such in the results.
- Fixed seed throughout, recorded in the corpus manifest.
- Metrics: equal error rate and a cost-weighted minimum detection cost for the
  voice branch, entity-level precision, recall and F1 with exact span matching
  for the tagger, area under ROC and per-cell decision accuracy for the fused
  system, Brier score and expected calibration error for calibration, and the
  time-to-detection distribution for the streaming path.
- Ablation arms are defined by feature subsets, so the comparison is like for
  like: `audio_only` sees the eight voice features, `text_only` the nine
  intent features, `late_fusion` those seventeen, and `full` all twenty-five.

## 6. Results

See `data/results/` and the console's Results Lab. The tables to read, in
order of what they settle:

1. **Ablation by cell** (`ablation_results.json`). The headline. Reported per
   cell rather than as a single aggregate, because a single number hides
   exactly the failure mode that matters: audio-only should fail on human
   scams, text-only should fail on mild scams and on cloned benign calls, and
   the full arm should clear both.
2. **Anti-spoofing** (`antispoof_results.json`). Five feature sets by two
   classical models by channel condition, with DET curves.
3. **Entity recognition** (`ner_results.json`). CRF against BiLSTM, gold
   transcripts against ASR transcripts.
4. **Intent** (`intent_results.json`). Rule baseline against TF-IDF.
5. **Robustness** (`robustness_results.json`). End-to-end AUC per codec.
6. **Time to detection** (`ttd_results.json`).
7. **Calibration** (`calibration.json`). This one is a precondition, not a
   footnote: late fusion over uncalibrated inputs tracks whichever branch is
   more confident rather than whichever is more correct.

The corpus is template-generated and therefore more regular than real speech,
so absolute numbers on it are optimistic. What the corpus can honestly settle
is the relative question, which is whether the cross-modal features add
anything over late fusion of two scores. Real-data numbers come from the Colab
notebooks on ASVspoof 2019 LA and the In-the-Wild set, and the console's
provenance table marks which is which.

## 7. Limitations

Written up in full in `docs/ETHICS.md`. In brief: offline numbers come from
generated audio; false positives on legitimate synthetic voices carry real
cost; Hinglish ASR is the weakest link in the chain and the system therefore
supports a transcript-in mode; and a determined attacker who is both a human
speaker and lexically careful defeats both branches. The claim is that
defeating both at once is harder, not that it is impossible.
